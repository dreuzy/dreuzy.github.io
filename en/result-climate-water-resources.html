Water resources and climate change

The climate signal is not directly the hydrological signal. Groundwater storage, geology, flow organization, reservoirs and water uses intervene between the two. Our work seeks to identify the hydrological variables that actually become limiting for territories.

Key result

Projections for the Rennes water-supply system show longer deficit periods, more consecutive dry years, lower reservoir refill and more constraining low flows — effects that cannot be read from precipitation changes alone.

Why use hydrological and hydrogeological models?

Available resources depend on recharge, storage, stream support and the initial state of the system. Two climatically similar years can have different consequences if they follow different hydrological sequences. Climate projections therefore have to be translated into functioning variables: groundwater levels, streamflow, intermittency, reservoir filling and low-flow duration.

Water resources around Rennes: thinking in trajectories

Models are driven by several climate projections to produce an envelope of possible futures rather than a single trajectory. The analysis focuses in particular on the duration of low-flow periods and sequences of deficit years.

The operational result is not simply “less water”: it concerns when specific functions become constrained — low-flow support, dilution of treated discharges, reservoir refill or interconnection between water-supply systems.

What the simulations highlight

Longer deficits

Periods when flows fall below historical thresholds lengthen through the century in the tested scenarios.

Multi-year memory

Sequences of dry years become decisive because stores do not always have time to recover.

Coupled constraints

Reservoir filling, stream low flows, treated discharges and water availability need to be considered together rather than separately.

The coast: another expression of the same problem

In Normandy, climate change also raises the inverse question: not only water scarcity, but vulnerability to rising groundwater and interactions between coastal aquifers, topography and sea level.

The approach remains similar: characterize system functioning, calibrate models against observations, then identify vulnerable areas and situations. The scientific question is how the subsurface transforms a regional forcing into very local consequences.

Scientific contribution

My contribution in this area is to combine parsimonious groundwater models, field data and climate scenarios to identify variables directly related to resource functioning. The work has developed with Alexandre Gauvain, Luc Aquilina, Bastien Boivin, June Sallou, colleagues at IRISA and water-resource managers in Brittany and Normandy.

Projects and extensions
