Scientific result 05 · mainly 2000–2015

From fracture networks to emergent properties

In fractured media, flow does not depend on mean permeability alone. Geometry, connectivity, shortcuts and dead ends organize preferential pathways that affect permeability, transport and reactivity in different ways.

Key result

Mean permeability can be related to a few statistical properties of the network, whereas channeling, transport and reactivity remain much more sensitive to the detailed organization of fracturing.

The upscaling problem

Natural networks contain fractures over a wide range of scales. Explicit representation rapidly leads to millions of cells and very large ensembles of possible realizations. The challenge is both to compute robustly on complex geometries and to identify which microscopic characteristics actually control larger-scale properties.

Mean permeability and flow organization tell different stories

Systematic simulations of 3D networks show that permeability is primarily related to the density of interconnected fractures and to an average of fracture permeabilities. But this statistical reduction no longer suffices for particle pathways or reactive transport.

Length distributions, connectivity, shortcuts, dead ends and aperture variations then strongly modulate channeling and transport times.

What this work contributed

1. Separating mean properties from path properties

A global property such as permeability can be summarized relatively well, whereas transport and reactivity retain strong memory of network organization.

2. Enabling statistical 3D studies

Numerical methods developed with Inria made it possible to simulate large ensembles of complex networks and systematically test upscaling rules.

3. Identifying channeling as a central object

Concentrated flows structure mixing, transit times and access to reactive surfaces. This idea later reappears in my work on shallow aquifers.

Methodological continuity with current research

Moving from fracture networks to catchments changes the objects but not the underlying question: which structures need to be retained to explain an emergent property? In current work, hillslope geometry, water-table position, seepage zones and reactive horizons play a role analogous to connectivity structures previously studied in fracture networks.

This continuity also explains the enduring importance of scientific computing, parsimonious models and multi-scale analysis in my research.

Scientific contribution

My main contribution concerned the relationship between fracture-network geometry, hydraulic properties and transport, together with the development of numerical methods robust enough to explore large network ensembles. This work was developed with Philippe Davy, Olivier Bour, Jocelyne Erhel, Géraldine Pichot, Tanguy Le Borgne, Delphine Roubinet, Romain Le Goc, Yves Méheust and several generations of PhD students and postdoctoral researchers.
